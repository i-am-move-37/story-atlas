# Genre scheme: candidate list (draft 2, 2026-11-05)

Status: **draft for Scott's review.** Nothing is locked. Draft 1 had 30 flat genres; draft 2 adds subgenres beneath them.

## What a genre means here
A genre is **a set of inherited conventions a writer works inside**: who the characters are, what situation they're in, how the story moves, how it ends. It isn't a shelf label, a format or an industry. A work can carry several labels. When it does, its atoms are scored against **each label's history separately**, never against the combined cell, so a mash-up like Brick (noir + teen) stays visible.

**Three levels.** Each subgenre sits under a genre, and each genre sits under a family. A work gets the finest labels that apply. When a subgenre has too few works in an era to have a meaningful history, the model falls back on its parent genre. A split survives only if labelers can apply it consistently (that gets measured in the labeling pilot).

- **Academic** (A): the film-studies genre canon (e.g. Neale, *Genre and Hollywood*), Moretti's British novel genres 1740–1900 (e.g. imperial gothic, silver-fork, Newgate), and Underwood's "Life Cycles of Genres".
- **Added by me** (C): genres none of the lists had but that I'd count as clear traditions. Each of these needs Scott's sign-off.

Letters after a name show which sources list it.

## Rules for what got left out
1. **Compounds of two genres are two labels, not one subgenre.** Action comedy, comedy horror, zombie comedy, sci-fi horror, sci-fi comedy, fantasy comedy, comedy thriller, crime thriller, crime drama, romantic thriller, fantasy romance, science fiction western, space western, gothic sci-fi, tech noir / future noir, horror drama, mafia comedy, comedy drama. Making them single labels would hide exactly the mash-ups we want to measure: a sci-fi western would no longer register as surprising. *Exception: romantic comedy, which is a tradition centuries old with its own conventions.*
2. **Formats and techniques:** all the animation types, documentary modes, anthology, serial, miniseries, sitcom, sketch, soap opera as a broadcast format, and all unscripted TV.
3. **Non-fiction:** biography, autobiography and memoir as literature, and documentary.
4. **Source-material labels:** video-game movies, film adaptation.
5. **Outside our date range (data ends about 2017):** LitRPG, progression fantasy, isekai.
6. **Hair-splits merged up:** the punk variants (clockpunk, mannerpunk, nanopunk, solarpunk, "~punk") fold into *retro-punk*, and two "contemporary fantasy" entries become one.
7. **Shelves:** Drama, Comedy, Fiction, Speculative (on their own), Art film, Experimental.

## Candidates raised during the labeling pilot
Not decided. Per call 5 above, nothing is added until the pilot is scored; this list collects what came up, with the evidence, for Scott to decide on afterward.

- **War by conflict: World War II, World War I, Vietnam** (Scott, 2026-11-10, while labeling). The current War subgenres are by story type (combat, home front, POW / escape, military drama, anti-war, resistance). Scott's view: each of these wars is its own genre. For: each has its own conventions (the Vietnam film's disillusionment and homecoming, the WWI story's trenches and futility, the WWII platoon and mission), and the scheme already has a precedent in "Cold War spy." To settle: whether a war film then takes two War labels (conflict and story type), and which other conflicts qualify. Evidence to gather: how many War works in the 200 would take each label.
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): add four subgenres under War, alongside the story-type ones: World War I, World War II, Vietnam, Holocaust.** A war film can carry one of each kind (*Gardens of Stone* = home front + Vietnam). Each has conventions of its own; film studies treats the World War II combat film as a genre (Basinger, *The World War II Combat Film: Anatomy of a Genre*, 1986). Subgenres and not genres because they then fall back on War's history wherever they are thin. Evidence: the corpus's existing labels can't size them (52 of 1,241 war-labeled works name World War II, 5 World War I, 8 the Holocaust, 1 Vietnam); of the 11 pilot works Scott labeled War, by Claude's reading six are World War II (three of them Holocaust stories), one World War I, one Vietnam. Left open: whether Holocaust belongs under War or Historical (many Holocaust stories are set after the war); and other conflicts (American Civil War, Napoleonic), which stay under plain War unless the corpus-wide labeling shows many works.

- **Animation and anime** (Scott asked, 2026-11-10, while labeling). Animation is excluded by rule 2 as a technique: an animated film takes the genres of its story. Anime is harder: it's a medium and an industry holding many genres, some of which the scheme has (magical girl, tokusatsu's neighbors, kaiju) and some it lacks. To settle: whether anime as a whole has conventions of its own, in the way J-horror does.
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): neither animation nor anime becomes a genre label.** Animation is how a film is made and a plot summary doesn't show it; an animated film's story elements belong in its story genres' histories. Anime is an industry and a visual style holding many genres (*Akira* and *My Neighbor Totoro* share little as stories), and the sources themselves sort it by story ("fantasy anime", "science fiction anime"). This matches call 2: a specific story tradition inside an industry stays (J-horror, tokusatsu, magical girl), the industry as a whole doesn't. Nothing is lost, since the sources already mark about 1,190 in-scope films as animated (4.7% of films) and about 280 as anime (1.1%), so results can still be split that way. Anime's own story traditions that the list lacks are judged one at a time, starting with mecha.
- **Mecha** (Claude noticed while answering the above). A long tradition with clear conventions (piloted giant robots, the reluctant young pilot), and absent from Science fiction.
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): add mecha as a subgenre of Science fiction.** Specific conventions (a machine that needs a human inside it, the young unwilling pilot, the robot handed down with strings attached, war fought by teenagers), and it is a story tradition inside an industry, like tokusatsu and kaiju. Small in our corpus (24 works labeled mecha by the sources; television series, where most mecha lives, are out of scope), which costs nothing because a thin subgenre falls back on its parent's history.

- **Literary fiction** (Scott asked, 2026-11-10). Left out as a shelf under rule 7: it's defined more by prestige and marketing than by story conventions, and its usual forms have labels (psychological drama, bildungsroman, campus novel, family saga, social realism, comedy of manners, magic realism). Against that: it arguably has conventions of its own (interior focus, style in the foreground, unresolved endings). Evidence to gather: how many pilot novels end up with "no genre fits" that Scott would have called literary fiction.
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): literary fiction stays out.** Like Drama it mostly says "serious, and not one of the named genres". What defines it (prose style, interior voice, ambiguity) is what a plot summary drops, and our story elements come from plot summaries. Its usual story forms have labels. Evidence: of the 59 novels Scott labeled in the pilot, none was left with no label, and his only "missing" note on a novel was *The Tunnel* ("post-modernist fiction?"), taken as its own item. The pilot novels lean to genre fiction (science fiction on 37, fantasy on 23).
- **Postmodern fiction** (Scott's note on *The Tunnel* in the pilot: "missing: post-modernist fiction?").
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): add metafiction as a subgenre of Weird & absurdist; do not add "postmodern".** Postmodern names a movement and a period, and most of what marks it (fragmented structure, pastiche, typographic play) disappears in a plot summary. Metafiction is the part that is a story convention: the author enters as a character, a character learns he is in a story, the story is about its own writing, the reader is addressed or offered several endings. It has a long line (*Tristram Shandy*, *Don Quixote* part two, Borges, Calvino, Fowles; in film *8½*, *Adaptation*, *Stranger than Fiction*). Evidence is thin (one pilot note; the sources label 17 of 9,884 in-scope novels metafiction and 19 postmodern); the decision rests on its having specific conventions, as with mecha, and a thin subgenre falls back on its parent.

- **Feminist fiction** (Scott asked, 2026-11-10). The scheme has only "feminist SF." The broader category is defined by theme and politics rather than by how the story moves, like the "LGBT-related" labels the crosswalk maps to nothing. But having feminist SF without it is inconsistent, so one of the two should change.
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): neither changes. Feminist SF stays; feminist fiction is not added.** The inconsistency is only on the surface. Feminist fiction is a theme: *The Awakening*, *The Color Purple* and *Thelma & Louise* share a concern, not a way the story moves, and each has a home in the genre whose shape it follows. Feminist SF is a set of story devices (the world without men, the people with no fixed sex, the state that controls childbearing), which is what a genre history can track; it stays for its conventions, as mecha and metafiction were added for theirs. Evidence: the sources label 34 in-scope films "feminist film" and about 10 novels feminist science fiction. **The same reasoning covers the sources' LGBT-related tag** (about 1,080 in-scope works, 3% of the corpus), which the crosswalk maps to no genre: it marks a subject spread across many genres. The tag stays in the data, so results can be split by it. A specific story shape inside it (the coming-out story, for example) would be judged on its own; none has been proposed.

- **Action comedy** (Scott, 2026-11-10, labeling *Metro*): feels as established as romantic comedy, the one compound that has its own label. For: a durable tradition since the early 1980s with its own conventions (the mismatched pair, banter under fire, comic set-piece violence). **A structural gap this exposed:** rule 1 turns a compound into two labels, but plain "comedy" is a shelf with no label, so every comedy compound (action comedy, comedy horror, sci-fi comedy, fantasy comedy, comedy thriller, comedy drama) keeps only its non-comedy half. Options: give the strongest comedy compounds their own labels; add a general comedy label for use in compounds; or leave the comic side to the tone facet of the atoms.
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): no label of its own; an action comedy is Action + Comedy.** The general Comedy genre (decided the same day, below) closes the structural gap. Two labels lose nothing, since works carrying both can be pulled out later to build an action-comedy history, while a combined label can't be split back; a combined label would also hide the crossing the study measures; and the mismatched-partners form already has "cop / buddy cop" and "buddy comedy". Romantic comedy stays the only compound with its own label: it is far older than film and has a plot shape of its own.

- **Drama and comedy** (found in the blind check, confirmed by Scott 2026-11-17). Scott: "I definitely felt the absence of drama and comedy throughout... melodrama kind of creeped into the category of general drama." In the check he starred Melodrama on *True Grit* (where it was a random decoy nobody had assigned), *Storm Over Asia*, *The Young Savages* and *Seedfolks*, none of which is a woman's film, family saga or tearjerker, and Romantic comedy on *Blonde and Blonder* and *You Again*, where the comedy isn't about a couple. Two costs: the narrow labels blur (Melodrama's history would mix the tradition with "any drama"), and methods are counted as missing a genre that isn't really there. This is the same gap as the action-comedy entry above, now on the drama side too. Options: add plain Drama and Comedy labels; keep them out and accept fewer labels on some works; keep them out and tighten the instructions so Melodrama and Romantic comedy mean only the narrow traditions. A fourth, Claude's lean and not decided: add both so a labeler has somewhere to put them, and decide separately whether they count as genre contexts when atoms are scored, since "Drama" as a context is close to the whole corpus.
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): add a general Comedy genre, with comedy-drama as a subgenre; do not add Drama.** Scott's question settled it: action comedy distinguishes among action films, but does adding "drama" distinguish anything? Almost never. Drama is the default reading of a genre and comedy is the marked case; where "X drama" is a real tradition the list already has it as a subgenre (legal, prison, military, romantic, teen, period, political, medical, psychological and religious drama, docudrama); and where drama seems to split a genre (science fiction drama against science fiction action) the split already shows as the presence or absence of Action or Adventure. Pilot evidence: the four works where melodrama stood in for drama each had two other starred genres, no pilot work ended with no label, and none of the three methods made the substitution. The one case where drama is the distinguishing half is comedy-drama, which becomes a subgenre of Comedy. The labeling instructions will say that Melodrama means the narrow tradition (woman's film, maternal melodrama, family saga, tearjerker). Claude had first recommended adding both and corrected that. To be applied to the scheme files with the other review decisions, in one edit at the end of the review.

- **Animal fantasy** (2026-11-10, labeling *Warriors: Into the Wild*; Scott and Claude reached the same labels independently). Animals with their own societies, laws and myths beside a human world: *Watership Down*, *Redwall*, *Warriors*. "Animal story" under Children's / family is much wider (it covers *Black Beauty*), and no Fantasy subgenre fits.
  - **Decided (Scott, 2026-11-17): add animal fantasy as a subgenre of Fantasy.** Conventions: an animal society with ranks, rules and a founding myth; an outsider who joins and earns a place; a journey to find or defend a home; humans as a force the animals can't make sense of. Animal story is wider (it covers animals living in the human world), fable uses animals to deliver a moral, and often there is no magic. The sources don't label it (the term appears on one work), but the works are in the corpus: *Watership Down* as novel and film, several *Redwall* and *Warriors* books, *The Plague Dogs*, *The Secret of NIMH*, *Tailchaser's Song*, *Guardians of Ga'Hoole*.

- **Bollywood / Hindi popular film** (Scott, 2026-11-10). "Masala film" covers only the 1970s–80s action-led mix; it doesn't fit a romance like *Tum Bin*, a period sports film like *Lagaan* or a family melodrama like *Kabhi Khushi Kabhie Gham*, which are labeled by story instead. What goes unlabeled is the form they share (song sequences as storytelling, the interval structure, the family-centered emotional register). Same question as anime: is an industry's shared form a genre? Indian films are a large share of the film corpus, so this one matters for coverage. Also: masala may be misfiled under Musical.
