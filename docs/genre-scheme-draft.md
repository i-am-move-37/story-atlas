# Genre scheme: candidate list (draft 2, 2026-11-05)

Status: **draft for Scott's review.** Nothing is locked. Draft 1 had 30 flat genres; draft 2 adds subgenres beneath them.

## What a genre means here
A genre is **a set of inherited conventions a writer works inside**: who the characters are, what situation they're in, how the story moves, how it ends. It isn't a shelf label, a format or an industry. A work can carry several labels. When it does, its atoms are scored against **each label's history separately**, never against the combined cell, so a mash-up like Brick (noir + teen) stays visible.

**Three levels.** Each subgenre sits under a genre, and each genre sits under a family. A work gets the finest labels that apply. When a subgenre has too few works in an era to have a meaningful history, the model falls back on its parent genre. A split survives only if labelers can apply it consistently (that gets measured in the labeling pilot).

Letters after a name show which sources list it.

## Rules for what got left out
1. **Compounds of two genres are two labels, not one subgenre.** Action comedy, comedy horror, zombie comedy, sci-fi horror, sci-fi comedy, fantasy comedy, comedy thriller, crime thriller, crime drama, romantic thriller, fantasy romance, science fiction western, space western, gothic sci-fi, tech noir / future noir, horror drama, mafia comedy, comedy drama. Making them single labels would hide exactly the mash-ups we want to measure: a sci-fi western would no longer register as surprising. *Exception: romantic comedy, which is a tradition centuries old with its own conventions.*
2. **Formats and techniques:** all the animation types, documentary modes, anthology, serial, miniseries, sitcom, sketch, soap opera as a broadcast format, and all unscripted TV.
3. **Non-fiction:** biography, autobiography and memoir as literature, and documentary.
4. **Source-material labels:** video-game movies, film adaptation.
5. **Outside our date range (data ends about 2017):** LitRPG, progression fantasy, isekai.
6. **Hair-splits merged up:** the punk variants (clockpunk, mannerpunk, nanopunk, solarpunk, "~punk") fold into *retro-punk*, and two "contemporary fantasy" entries become one.
7. **Shelves:** Drama, Comedy, Fiction, Speculative (on their own), Art film, Experimental.

## Scott's calls (2026-11-05)
1. **Romantic comedy is the only compound with its own label.** Science fantasy is therefore removed from Fantasy and labeled as science fiction + fantasy instead.
2. **National and industry cycles stay** (spaghetti western, giallo, poliziotteschi, masala, commedia all'italiana, tokusatsu, J-horror). They start in an era but outlive it.
3. **Found footage and mockumentary stay.**
4. **My additions (C) stay as they are.**
5. **Nothing more gets added for now**; the labeling pilot will show what's missing.

These are draft decisions. The scheme is frozen only when the research plan is committed.

## Candidates raised during the labeling pilot
Not decided. Per call 5 above, nothing is added until the pilot is scored; this list collects what came up, with the evidence, for Scott to decide on afterward.

- **War by conflict: World War II, World War I, Vietnam** (Scott, 2026-11-10, while labeling). The current War subgenres are by story type (combat, home front, POW / escape, military drama, anti-war, resistance). Scott's view: each of these wars is its own genre. For: each has its own conventions (the Vietnam film's disillusionment and homecoming, the WWI story's trenches and futility, the WWII platoon and mission), and the scheme already has a precedent in "Cold War spy." To settle: whether a war film then takes two War labels (conflict and story type), and which other conflicts qualify. Evidence to gather: how many War works in the 200 would take each label.

- **Animation and anime** (Scott asked, 2026-11-10, while labeling). Animation is excluded by rule 2 as a technique: an animated film takes the genres of its story. Anime is harder: it's a medium and an industry holding many genres, some of which the scheme has (magical girl, tokusatsu's neighbors, kaiju) and some it lacks. To settle: whether anime as a whole has conventions of its own, in the way J-horror does.
- **Mecha** (Claude noticed while answering the above). A long tradition with clear conventions (piloted giant robots, the reluctant young pilot), and absent from Science fiction.

- **Literary fiction** (Scott asked, 2026-11-10). Left out as a shelf under rule 7: it's defined more by prestige and marketing than by story conventions, and its usual forms have labels (psychological drama, bildungsroman, campus novel, family saga, social realism, comedy of manners, magic realism). Against that: it arguably has conventions of its own (interior focus, style in the foreground, unresolved endings). Evidence to gather: how many pilot novels end up with "no genre fits" that Scott would have called literary fiction.
- **Feminist fiction** (Scott asked, 2026-11-10). The scheme has only "feminist SF." The broader category is defined by theme and politics rather than by how the story moves, like the "LGBT-related" labels the crosswalk maps to nothing. But having feminist SF without it is inconsistent, so one of the two should change.

- **Action comedy** (Scott, 2026-11-10, labeling *Metro*): feels as established as romantic comedy, the one compound that has its own label. For: a durable tradition since the early 1980s with its own conventions (the mismatched pair, banter under fire, comic set-piece violence). **A structural gap this exposed:** rule 1 turns a compound into two labels, but plain "comedy" is a shelf with no label, so every comedy compound (action comedy, comedy horror, sci-fi comedy, fantasy comedy, comedy thriller, comedy drama) keeps only its non-comedy half. Options: give the strongest comedy compounds their own labels; add a general comedy label for use in compounds; or leave the comic side to the tone facet of the atoms.

- **Animal fantasy** (2026-11-10, labeling *Warriors: Into the Wild*; Scott and Claude reached the same labels independently). Animals with their own societies, laws and myths beside a human world: *Watership Down*, *Redwall*, *Warriors*. "Animal story" under Children's / family is much wider (it covers *Black Beauty*), and no Fantasy subgenre fits.

- **Bollywood / Hindi popular film** (Scott, 2026-11-10). "Masala film" covers only the 1970s–80s action-led mix; it doesn't fit a romance like *Tum Bin*, a period sports film like *Lagaan* or a family melodrama like *Kabhi Khushi Kabhie Gham*, which are labeled by story instead. What goes unlabeled is the form they share (song sequences as storytelling, the interval structure, the family-centered emotional register). Same question as anime: is an industry's shared form a genre? Indian films are a large share of the film corpus, so this one matters for coverage. Also: masala may be misfiled under Musical.
