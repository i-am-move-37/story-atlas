# Design: Story Atlas

## Purpose
Gather as many plot summaries as possible (TV series and episodes, films, novels) as raw material for story-atom extraction and idea generation. Plot summaries come first; credits are kept when they come free with the same request.

Started 2026-11-02, following the Lacuna / Alien Space of Science discussion and the West Wing graph explorer (`../westwing-kg`).

## Decisions
- **TMDB for TV.** No good TV plot dataset exists, so we pull TMDB: series overviews plus per-episode overviews. Episode writers and directors come with the season call.
- **Whole catalog, not just dramas.** The ID export lists all ~236k series. Every series is fetched (type and genre are only known after the details call). Seasons are fetched only for `Scripted` and `Miniseries`.
- **Films from TMDB too,** limited to export popularity ≥ 1 (~462k of 1.27M), since the long tail is mostly empty stubs. TMDB film overviews are short blurbs; the Wikipedia datasets have full plots for ~40k films.
- **Trimmed JSONL, resumable.** Only story-relevant fields are kept. Append-only JSONL with skip-if-saved makes crashes cheap.
- **Pace.** 48 worker threads, capped at 40 req/s (TMDB allows ~50). Requests take ~1s over the VPN, so concurrency sets the speed: ~34/s in practice.
- **Join keys.** IMDb and Wikidata IDs are kept, to link TMDB to TV Tropes (IMDb) and the Wikipedia plot sets (Wikidata).

## Combined table decisions
- **One flat table, every source.** Columns: uid, source, medium, title, series_title, season, episode, year, language, genres, creators, words, text, plus join IDs. `creators` means authors (books), directors and writers (films), creators (series) or episode writers.
- **Empty summaries dropped.** Whitespace collapsed; `words` is a plain split count.
- **No deduplication.** The same film can appear up to four times (TMDB, CMU, Wiki Movie Plots, WikiPlots). The source is kept so either can be chosen; dedupe later via Wikidata/IMDb IDs or titles plus year.
- **WikiPlots medium.** Read from the title tag (film, novel, play, game, "(Show name)" → episode). Untagged titles are matched by exact title against CMU book titles, then Wikipedia film titles, then English TMDB series names. Unknowns fell from 79k to 40k. The matching can mislabel a title shared by a book and a film; book wins.
- **DuckDB for search.** It scans the Parquet file directly, so there's no loading step or index to maintain.

## Gap-finding study (direction set 2026-11-04, still exploratory)
Adapt the Alien Space of Science method (arXiv 2603.01092; code at alejandrohdez00/alien-space-of-science) to stories: extract story atoms, find combinations that are coherent but unavailable, decode them into premises. Aim: academic rigor, possibly a write-up.
- **Corpus:** films and novels with long plots only (about 52.7k films and 13.9k novels at 300+ words before dedupe).
- **Availability = genre and era, not writers.** Brick (genre-modernizing) is the touchstone example.
- **Models written by hand,** with Claude guiding step by step; the learning is part of the goal. A counting baseline (pair co-occurrence) is what the model must beat.
- **Leak to avoid:** the atom vocabulary must be built only from works before the temporal cutoff.
- Live tracker of the steps: https://claude.ai/artifact/1w18HGqmEcGbxhRLjxraHY (steps live in its database, collection `steps`).
- Their released data (HF `alejandrohdez/alien-data`, copy in the session scratchpad): about 5.1 extracted units per paper; atoms per paper average 1.65 in the original clustering (`clusters.json`) and 3.38 after noise reassignment (`clusters_80.json`). Their own prompts (blog, extract, rate and repair, cluster naming) are in `atomization/prompts.py`.
- **Caution, their release:** in `clusters_80.json` the atom labels don't match that file's own atom names (71 of 16,004 sample atoms sit under their own label, against 16,004 of 16,004 in `clusters.json`), and the coherence training data uses the `clusters_80` labels. Use `clusters.json` for worked examples; not yet checked whether their decoded proposals were affected.
- **Safe peeks (2026-11-04)**, on films and novels with 300+ words, using a rough dedupe by normalized title (years within 1). Script: session scratchpad `peeks.py`.
  - Dedupe: 52,714 film rows come down to about 25.6k works, since half the rows are copies. 13,915 novel rows come down to about 8.7k works.
  - WikiPlots rows have no year or genre in our table. About 11k film titles carry a year in the raw tag ("(1979 film)"), which the build drops. Novels never have one. Undated after dedupe: 2.4k films and 3.4k novels (40% of novels).
  - Per decade, films: 1930s about 1k, 1940s through 1970s about 1.4–1.5k each, 1980s 2.3k, 1990s 3.3k, 2000s 6.1k, 2010s 4.2k. Novels peak in the 2000s at 1.6k; CMU books stop at 2012. Before 2000 there are about 12.8k films and 3.5k novels; from 2000 on, about 10.4k films and 1.8k novels.
  - Length drifts by era for films: the median rises from about 465 words in the 1920s to 667 in the 2010s, and the share at 600+ words rises from 27% to 61%. Novels stay flat at about 650–730. This confound needs handling, since longer summaries likely yield more atoms.
  - Genres: three schemes that don't match. CMU film has 344 labels, 4.4 per film. Wiki Movie Plots has 646 free-text labels, 1.2 per film. CMU book has 204 labels, dominated by "Fiction" and "Speculative fiction". About 21.3k of 25.6k films and 6.1k of 8.7k novels have any label.
- **Splits (2026-11-05, leaning):** use both. A random split, grouped so that remakes, adaptations and franchise entries stay on one side, is the main check that the coherence model works; it also keeps the undated works. A temporal split on the dated subset is a secondary, more ambitious claim.
- **Genre scheme (2026-11-05, draft):** genre = a set of inherited conventions, not a shelf or format. It's multi-label and shared across film and novel. A multi-genre work's atoms are scored against each genre separately, never the joint cell, so mash-ups like Brick (noir + teen) stay visible; rarely combined genre pairs also make a counting baseline. Candidate list (30 genres in 7 families), sizing and open questions: `docs/genre-scheme-draft.md`. Finding: the source labels badly under-count fine genres (e.g. 185 gangster films), so the labeling source is open: Wikidata, an LLM given metadata only, or an LLM given the plot.
  - Scott's view (2026-11-05): all 30 are true genres. Split finer (100+ subgenres exist) wherever labels can be applied reliably. Audience genres count. For labels, use whatever gives the most coverage that's likely correct. Proposed: two levels (subgenres under ~30 parents, backing off to the parent when a cell is thin); split until labelers stop agreeing; pick the labeling source with a small measured pilot.
  - Draft 2 (2026-11-05): three levels, 7 families → 44 genres → 218 subgenres. It merges Wikipedia's List of genres, Boords' 100 movie genres, NYFA's film sub-genres and the academic sources, with my additions marked for review. Key rule: **compounds of two genres become two labels** (action comedy, sci-fi western, tech noir...), so mash-ups stay measurable; romantic comedy is the one exception so far. Also out: formats, non-fiction, source-material labels, post-2017 web-fiction genres.
  - Scott's calls on draft 2 (2026-11-05): romantic comedy is the only compound with its own label (so science fantasy is removed; 217 subgenres). National and industry cycles stay, since they start in an era but outlive it. Found footage and mockumentary stay. My additions stay. Nothing more gets added until the labeling pilot shows gaps.
  - **Era bins (2026-11-05):** decades, from 1910 on for films and from 1900 on for novels. Earlier years go in wide bins (e.g. one per half-century for 19th-century novels, one bin before 1800). A thin subgenre × decade cell backs off to neighboring decades and to the parent genre, the same mechanism as the genre hierarchy. If boundary effects matter, the fallback is year as a continuous variable with smoothing.
  - **Test cutoff (2026-11-05): 2005.** That trains on about 15.3k films and 4.2k novels and tests on 2005–2017, about 7.9k films and 1.1k novels (novels thin out after 2010; CMU books stop in 2012). Novels are the binding constraint; a cutoff later than about 2008 leaves too few test novels. If the corpus is later extended to the present with a fresh Wikipedia pull, revisit (Scott's first instinct was 2015).
  - **Rehearsal cutoffs (2026-11-05): 1972, 1982, 1992.** Each mirrors the real test's 12-year window (1972–83, 1982–93, 1992–2003), and every window ends before 2005. They're used for all tuning; the 2005 test runs once.
  - Evidence: with the coarse 30 and today's sparse labels, 395 of 435 genre pairs already occur at least once, but only 1,673 of 4,060 triples do. Noir + teen occurs in 3 works: Brick, the YA novel *Silent to the Bone*, and *Bad Education* (a false hit via "Coming of age").
- **Research plan draft:** `docs/research-plan.md` (started 2026-11-06). Section 1 (question and hypotheses) is drafted and its decisions are made (2026-11-06): H3 (human interest) is primary; H1 (coherence) and H2 (temporal recovery) are confirmatory supports; H4 (reception variance) is exploratory; a win is a 95% interval excluding zero, with the effect size reported; Holm correction across H1–H3. Section 2 (corpus and temporal design) is drafted (2026-11-06): length drift handled by a standard-length rewrite plus an atom cap per work, checked by decade; a labeling pilot of about 200 works with a gold set from Scott (50 from scratch, 150 checked) and a fixed selection rule; grouped random split (remakes, adaptations, franchises grouped; loose retellings deliberately not); post-2005 remakes, sequels and adaptations can't count as H2 hits. Section 3 (atom vocabulary) is drafted (2026-11-09): **typed atoms in ten facets** (setting, protagonist, want tagged internal/external, need with "none" allowed, antagonist, inciting situation, conflict engine, structural device, tone, ending shape including how the need resolves). Want and need were Scott's additions. Atoms must cite supporting text; extract once, cluster per run; post-cutoff atoms matching no cluster are counted as new building blocks; vocabulary size chosen by the best downstream score among clusterings passing random- and nearest-neighbor intruder tests; extraction pilot on about 100 pre-1972 works. Sections 4–6 are stubs.
- **Baselines (Scott's calls, 2026-11-09; section 4 not yet written):** the ladder is (1) random coherent combinations, (2) pair counting, (3) a simple learned model, (4) trend extrapolation for H2 (combinations already rising before the cutoff), (5) asking an LLM directly for H3. The controlled version of (5) has the LLM pick atoms from our vocabulary and the shared decoder write the premise; it's a third arm of H3, and the free-form version is exploratory. H2's random comparison set is matched on atom frequency as well as coherence. Tuning budget: an equal number of configurations per method (about 30), all chosen on the rehearsals by the same metric, and every configuration logged. Scott wants hands-on model training out of this project, which shapes which components get trained rather than called through an API.
  - **What Scott trains (2026-11-10): the coherence model, as a small transformer trained from scratch** on atom tokens with a masked-atom objective; and **a distilled extractor**, a small open LLM fine-tuned with LoRA on frontier-model extractions of pre-2005 works, used only if it comes within 5 points of the teacher (proposed). DPO on the decoder is kept out of the confirmatory pipeline, since it risks contaminating H3 if it learns the judges' taste.
  - **Section 4 drafted (2026-11-10):** the baseline ladder, with each arm mapped to H1/H2/H3; the LLM arms are kept out of H2 because the LLM has read post-2005 works. Configurations are run with 3 seeds each, and seeds don't count against the budget. The H3 arms' free settings are tuned by one fixed LLM-judge interest proxy (proposed). Scott's calls (2026-11-10): H3 succeeds if the model beats counting and random. "Beats LLM direct" is a separate pre-registered test, H3b, so Holm now covers four tests. The linear model is secondary. One LLM judge tunes every arm's free setting, with two safeguards: it comes from a different model family than the LLM arm, and it's checked against human ratings first, with a fallback to fixing the settings by hand. Section 4 has no open decisions. An "arm" means one source of premises in the human evaluation.
  - **Section 5 drafted (2026-11-10), with Scott's calls:** gap score = coherence − β × availability, both from **one transformer asked two ways** (without genre and decade tags for coherence, with them for availability; tags dropped at random in training). H1's number: how often a method scores a real held-out work above a half-scrambled copy. **H2's unit is a genre plus a pair of atoms never seen together in that genre before the cutoff**; each method names its top 1,000 and the number is how many appear in the next 12 years. H3 and H3b: win rate in blind head-to-head comparisons. Configurations are scored by the H2 number averaged over the three rehearsals. One-shot run: freeze, no statistics on post-2005 atoms before the freeze, run once, report everything; reruns only for bugs demonstrable without the results. After the test, the frozen configuration is retrained on the whole corpus to write the H3 premises (proposed).
  - **H2's floor changed (Scott, 2026-11-10).** The old floor, random combinations "of equal coherence," would tie with the model by construction if the rehearsals choose β near zero, which is likely if near-genre combinations are the ones that get filled. The confirmatory floor is now random never-done combinations matched on atom frequency only; the coherence-matched comparison is secondary. H2 now reads: among combinations never done in a genre before 2005, the model's top picks get done more often than the floor's, counting's and trend extrapolation's. H3's random arm stays matched on coherence. Sections 1, 4.2 and 5.3 were updated; section 5 has no open decisions.
  - **Section 6 drafted (2026-11-10):** threats in three groups (handled by the design; handled by a new check; accepted limits). New checks: a blind audit of 100 H2 hits per method (atoms present, genre fits, not a remake); **core/borderline marks on genre labels**, with H2 rerun on core labels only (Scott's point: Brick could defensibly be called a neo-western); a **summary-stability check** (atoms extracted from two versions of about 300 films' summaries, agreement per facet; Scott's point: summaries contain errors and subjective calls); a novelty screen on every premise; results split by film and novel; Scott doesn't judge H3; the distilled extractor is checked on a post-2005 sample. Accepted limits include "never done" meaning "never done in our corpus," and hindsight in the extraction LLM and embedding model. Also fixed an inconsistency: section 2.2's atom cap now matches 3.2 (at most *m* per facet). **All six sections are drafted with no open decisions**; several values remain marked "proposed."
  - **Walkthrough, step 1 (corpus and labels), 2026-11-10.** A word-for-word record is kept in `docs/walkthrough-notes.md`, one section per step (Scott's request). Changes it produced: summaries are not merged (longest kept); Scott labels the work itself for the gold set, not just its summary; the 150-work draft is the three methods' labels pooled and unattributed; the subgenre merging rule became two-part, because 200 pilot works can't give a meaningful κ for most of 217 subgenres (gold-set κ where there are at least 5 examples; κ between the two best automatic methods across the corpus otherwise). The 90% coverage, 2-point tie and κ 0.4 values were reviewed and stand.
